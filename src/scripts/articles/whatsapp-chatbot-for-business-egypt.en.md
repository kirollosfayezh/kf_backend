For many businesses in Egypt, WhatsApp is the real front desk. Customers ask
about prices at midnight, send "is this available?" all day, and want to know
where their order is. Every slow reply is a customer who might buy elsewhere.

A WhatsApp chatbot can take much of that load. Set up the wrong way, it can also
annoy people or get your number banned.

## What a WhatsApp bot can and can't do

A bot is good at questions with a clear answer that come up again and again.

**It can usually handle:**

- Opening hours, location, delivery areas and fees
- Prices and availability, if they come from a list or a system it can read
- Order status, if it is connected to wherever orders are recorded
- Bookings and appointments, with confirmation and reminders
- Collecting details before a human takes over

**It should not be trusted with:**

- Complaints and angry customers, who want a person, not a menu
- Negotiation, special requests and anything that needs judgement
- Medical, legal or financial advice
- Anything where a wrong answer costs real money

The goal is not to remove people. It is to stop them typing the same five
answers all day.

## The official API or an unofficial tool

**The WhatsApp Business app** is free, with greeting and away messages, quick
replies and a catalogue. For a small shop, that may be enough to start.

**The WhatsApp Business Platform (the API)** is Meta's official route for
automation. You access it directly through Meta or through an approved provider.
It needs a verified business account in Meta Business Manager and a phone number
registered for it. Messages you send first, outside the customer service window,
must use templates Meta approves.

**Unofficial tools** automate a normal WhatsApp account, usually by controlling
WhatsApp Web. They are cheap, and they break WhatsApp's terms of service.
WhatsApp can restrict or ban numbers it detects being automated. If that is the
number on your shop sign and in every ad, losing it is a serious problem.

> If your business depends on one WhatsApp number, do not put that number on a
> tool that breaks the rules.

Meta's terms also restrict using the platform for general-purpose AI
assistants. A bot that answers questions about your own business, products and
orders is what the platform is meant for.

## What to automate first

Go through a week of chats and count what your team answers most. The list
usually looks like this:

1. **FAQs**: hours, location, delivery, payment methods, return policy.
2. **Prices and the catalogue**: the product list, with photos and prices that
   stay up to date.
3. **Order status**: the customer sends an order number and gets the status.
   This needs a connection to your order system, covered in
   [connecting business systems through APIs](/blog/api-integration-connecting-business-systems).
4. **Booking**: available slots, confirmation and a reminder the day before.
5. **Handing over to a human**: a clear "talk to a person" option on every menu,
   and the conversation passed to your team with what the customer already said.

The last one is not optional. A bot with no way out loses customers who were
ready to buy.

## Rule-based or AI

**A rule-based bot** works with buttons and menus: "Press 1 for prices". It is
predictable and cheaper to build and run, but it handles typed questions badly,
and people often type.

**An AI bot** understands free text in Egyptian Arabic, English or a mix, and
answers from your own information: price lists, policies, product details. It
feels natural, but it can give a confident wrong answer if it is not restricted
to your data and tested. It also costs something per conversation.

Good setups often combine the two: menus for common tasks, AI for open
questions, and strict rules for prices, refunds or promises. The approach in
[adding AI to your business](/blog/a-practical-guide-to-adding-ai-to-your-business)
applies here too: test on real conversations before trusting it with customers.

## What drives the cost

We will not give one number without knowing the setup. These move it:

- **Official platform fees**: Meta charges for some message types, mainly
  templates the business sends first, at rates that vary by country. Replies
  inside the customer service window are generally free. Pricing changes, so
  check Meta's current rates.
- **The provider**, if you use one: often a monthly subscription, sometimes with a
  per-message margin.
- **Rule-based or AI**: AI adds a usage cost and testing time.
- **Integrations**: connecting to your order system, booking calendar, CRM or
  stock. Each one is real work.
- **The team inbox**: several agents need a shared inbox with history, not one
  phone passed around.

## How to start small

1. **Collect real questions** from a few weeks of chats and rank them.
2. **Automate the top three to five** only, with clear answers you have agreed on.
3. **Add the handover** to a person from day one.
4. **Run it alongside your team** and read the conversations where it failed.
5. **Add one capability at a time**, such as order status, once the first part
   works.

## Common questions

### Can I keep my current WhatsApp number?

Usually, but moving a number to the official platform can change how you use it
in the normal app. Check with your provider first, and consider testing on a
second number.

### Will customers know they are talking to a bot?

They should. Say so in the first message and offer a person at any point. People
accept an honest bot far more readily than one pretending to be human.

### Can the bot send offers to all my customers?

Only to customers who agreed to receive messages from you, and only with
approved templates on the official platform. Mass messages to people who did not
opt in get reported, and reports hurt your number's quality rating.

### How long does it take to set up?

A simple FAQ bot on the official platform can be running within a few weeks,
much of it spent on business verification and agreeing the answers. Connecting
order or booking systems adds time.
