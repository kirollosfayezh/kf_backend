Most shop owners choose a cashier system the same way: a salesperson rings up
three items on a tablet, it looks fast, and the decision is made. The problems
show up a month later, on the first busy evening with a return, a shift change
and a customer who wants to pay the rest next week.

A supermarket, a clothing shop, a phone shop and a pharmacy need different
things from a POS, but they fail in the same places. Here is how to choose one
that holds up after the demo.

## Start with how you actually sell

Before you look at any system, write down three things:

- **How many items you carry**, and whether they already have barcodes.
- **Whether items have variants.** Clothing has sizes and colours. Phones have
  serial numbers and IMEIs. Pharmacies have batches and expiry dates. A system
  built for one of these rarely handles the others well.
- **How many branches and cashiers**, now and in two years.

These three answers eliminate most of the market before the first demo.

## The must-have checklist

### Barcode and fast item lookup

Scanning should add an item instantly, and search by name or code should work
when a label is missing. The system should also print barcode labels for items
that arrive without one.

### Inventory that updates with every sale

Every sale, return and delivery should move the stock figure. Ask how it handles
stock counts and damaged goods. If the answer involves a spreadsheet, it does not.

### Returns and exchanges

Returns are where cash goes missing. The system should link a return to the
original receipt, put the item back in stock and record who approved it. An
exchange for a different size should be one operation, not a return plus a new
sale typed by hand.

### Shifts and the cash drawer

Each cashier opens a shift with a starting float and closes it with a count. The
system shows what should be in the drawer, the cashier enters what is there, and
the difference is recorded against that shift. Without this, a shortage cannot
be traced.

### Multi-branch stock

If you run more than one shop, you need one stock figure per item across all of
them, transfers between branches, and the ability to see what is available
elsewhere while a customer waits.

### Customer accounts and credit

A lot of Egyptian retail runs on regular customers who pay later. The system
should hold customer balances, record partial payments and show who owes what,
so the credit notebook next to the till can finally go.

### Reports you will actually read

Daily sales, sales by cashier, best and slow sellers, stock value and low-stock
alerts. The test is simple: can you answer "what did I sell yesterday, and what
do I need to reorder?" in under a minute?

## ETA e-receipts

If you sell to consumers, Egypt's e-receipt system is part of your POS decision,
not something to add later. Every sale has to be submitted to the Tax Authority
with the right item codes and tax breakdown, and the printed receipt normally
carries the identifier that comes back.

Ask any vendor whether e-receipt works today, not whether it is planned, and ask
to see a submitted receipt. The details of what the integration involves are in
[connecting your POS to the ETA system](/blog/eta-e-invoicing-pos-integration).

## Offline mode is not optional

When the internet drops, the shop has to keep selling. A good POS stores sales
locally, prints receipts, and syncs everything, e-receipts included, once the
connection returns.

Test this yourself during the demo: disconnect the internet, make three sales and
a return, then reconnect. If the vendor hesitates to let you try, you have your
answer.

## Hardware

Every checkout needs the same three devices:

- **Receipt printer**, usually 80 mm thermal
- **Barcode scanner**, wired or wireless
- **Cash drawer**, usually opened by the printer

Before buying, confirm the software supports those exact models, and that
spare parts and service are available locally.

## Cloud or local

A **local** system runs on a computer in the shop: fast and independent of the
internet, but backups and remote access are your problem. A **cloud** system lets
you check sales from your phone and gives branches one shared database, but it
needs a solid offline mode to be safe at the counter.

For most multi-branch retail, the practical answer is both: cloud data, with each
checkout able to run on its own when the connection drops.

## Ready-made subscription or custom

Subscription POS apps are the right choice for most single shops that sell in
a normal way: quick to set up and cheap to start.

A custom system makes sense when the ready-made options force workarounds every
day: serial-number tracking for phones, batch and expiry control, unusual credit
arrangements, or several branches that must connect to your accounting. We
worked through the same decision for restaurants in
[ready-made or custom POS](/blog/ready-made-vs-custom-pos-restaurants), and the
logic carries over to retail.

## What drives the cost

- **Checkouts and branches**, since most subscriptions charge per device or branch
- **Hardware**, multiplied by every checkout
- **E-receipt compliance**, which some vendors include and others charge for
- **Data migration** of items and customer balances
- **Integrations** with accounting, an online store or delivery apps
- **Training and support**

Compare the total over three years, not the monthly fee.

## Red flags when buying

- No working offline mode, or a vague answer about it
- E-receipt is "coming soon"
- You cannot export your own data without asking the vendor
- Prices change sharply once you add a second branch
- No local support, or support only by email

## Common questions

### Can one POS work for a supermarket and a clothing shop?

It can, but check the specific features each needs. A supermarket needs fast
scanning and weighed items, while a clothing shop needs sizes, colours and easy
exchanges. A system strong at one is often weak at the other, so test it with
your own products.

### Do I need an internet connection to use a POS?

You need it to sync data and submit e-receipts, but you should not need it to
sell. A good system keeps working offline and catches up automatically when the
connection returns.

### How long does it take to move to a new POS?

A ready-made system can run within days if your item list is clean. The slow
part is usually preparing the data: barcodes, prices, opening stock and customer
balances. Plan that before you pick a start date.
