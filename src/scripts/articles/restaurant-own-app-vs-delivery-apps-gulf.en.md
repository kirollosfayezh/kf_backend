For most Gulf restaurants the answer is not one or the other: stay on the delivery apps for new customers, and add your own ordering app once you have enough regulars to move. The platforms bring discovery and drivers that your own app cannot replace. Your own app brings what the platforms keep for themselves: the customer's details, a direct relationship, your own loyalty scheme and payment straight to you.

Last checked: 6 November 2026

## Who delivers where in the Gulf

The platforms below are the ones we confirmed on each company's own website or official announcement. It is not a complete list, and local apps come and go, so check your city before you plan.

Two changes are worth knowing. Keeta, the international delivery brand of China's Meituan, now has country sites for Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait, Qatar and Bahrain, so most Gulf markets have one more large platform than they did two years ago. And DoorDash completed its purchase of Deliveroo on 2 October 2025; Deliveroo keeps its own brand. Neither change alters the basic trade-off below, but both mean the terms you signed a few years ago may not be the terms you would get today.

## What the delivery apps give, and what they keep

It is easy to resent the commission and forget what it buys. The platforms give you:

- **Discovery.** Hungry people open a delivery app before they think of a restaurant name. Being listed puts you in front of customers who have never heard of you.
- **Drivers.** You do not hire, insure or schedule riders, and you do not pay for idle time on a slow afternoon.
- **Payments and support.** The platform takes the payment, handles most complaints and refunds, and pays you out.
- **Demand data at the area level.** You see which dishes sell, at what times, in which districts.

For a new restaurant, or one testing a new area, this is hard to beat. Building your own app first and hoping people find it is a common and expensive mistake.

### What the platforms keep for themselves

The cost of a platform is not only the commission on each order. Ask for your own rates in writing, because they are negotiated and differ by restaurant, city and plan. Beyond the rate, three things stay with the platform:

- **The customer.** On an aggregator, the person who ordered is the platform's customer. You usually see a first name and an order, not a phone number or an email address you can use for your own marketing.
- **The ranking.** Where you appear in the list depends on the platform's algorithm, its paid placements and its own promotions. A competitor who pays for visibility can push you down.
- **The price your customer sees.** Platform fees, minimum order rules and delivery charges shape what the customer pays. Many restaurants respond by raising menu prices on the apps, which makes their own channel look cheaper.

None of this is a reason to leave the platforms. It is a reason not to depend on them for every order.

## What your own ordering app adds

An app with your name on it changes who owns the relationship.

- **Customer data you own.** Every order gives you a name, phone number, address and order history in your own database, subject to the data-protection law of the country you serve.
- **A loyalty scheme on your terms.** Points, stamps, a free dessert on the fifth order, birthday offers: you decide the rules and you pay for them, rather than funding platform-wide discounts.
- **Direct payment.** Customers pay you through a licensed payment gateway such as Tap, PayTabs or Telr, using the methods they already use: mada cards and Apple Pay in Saudi Arabia, KNET in Kuwait, BenefitPay in Bahrain, plus international cards everywhere. The money reaches your account without a platform in between.
- **Push notifications to your regulars.** A new dish, a weekend offer or a reminder to people who have not ordered in a month, sent without paying per message.
- **Pickup and dine-in, not only delivery.** Order ahead for collection, order from the table by scanning a code, or pre-order for a set time. The platforms are built around delivery; your app can be built around how your restaurant actually works.
- **Your menu and your prices.** No minimum-order rules you did not set, and the same price in the app as at the counter if you choose.

## What your own app cannot do

Be honest about the limits before you spend anything.

- **It will not find new customers by itself.** Nobody searches the app store for a restaurant they do not know. Your app grows from people who already like your food: dine-in guests, regulars, people who ordered once through a platform and came back.
- **It does not come with drivers.** You need your own riders, a third-party fleet, or pickup only. Each has a cost and needs someone to manage it.
- **It needs a reason to install.** A customer will not download a second app to get the same price. Loyalty points, app-only offers or faster pickup have to make the switch worth it.
- **It needs looking after.** Menus change, phones update, payment providers update. Budget for maintenance, not only for the first build.

## The setup most restaurants end up with

The practical answer is both channels, connected to one kitchen.

1. **Stay on the platforms that matter in your city** for discovery and occasional customers.
2. **Launch your own app or ordering website** and promote it to the people already in front of you: table cards, receipts, packaging inserts, your Instagram and WhatsApp.
3. **Give a clear reason to order direct**: points, a lower price than on the platforms, or a perk that only exists in your app.
4. **Bring every order into one place.** Platform orders, app orders and dine-in tickets should land in the same POS and the same kitchen screen. If staff retype orders from tablets, mistakes and delays follow.

The fourth step is where many projects go wrong. Before you build anything, find out whether your POS has a documented way to receive orders from outside, and whether your platforms can send orders into it. ## When building your own app makes sense

Use this as a rough test. The more of these are true, the stronger the case.

- You have a base of regulars who order at least a few times a month.
- A meaningful share of your platform orders come from repeat customers you could reach directly.
- You have, or can arrange, delivery riders or a strong pickup trade.
- You run more than one branch, or plan to, so one app serves them all.
- You want a loyalty scheme or offers that the platforms will not run for you.
- Your POS can accept orders from outside, or you are ready to change it.

If most of these are false, start smaller: a fast ordering website with your menu and a direct payment link costs less, needs no store approval, and tells you whether people will order direct before you invest in a full app. Our article on [whether a restaurant should build its own ordering and delivery app](/blog/restaurant-delivery-app-egypt) walks through the same decision with the Egyptian market in mind; most of the reasoning carries over.

## Common questions

### Will my own app replace the delivery apps?

No, and it should not try to. The platforms bring customers who do not know you yet. Your app is for the ones who do. Most restaurants that run their own app keep their platform listings.

### Can I charge less in my own app than on the platforms?

That depends on your contract with each platform, which can include terms on pricing. Read yours, or ask the platform, before you advertise a lower direct price.

### Do I need a full app, or is an ordering website enough?

For many restaurants a mobile ordering website is the better first step. It opens from a link or a QR code with nothing to install. A full app earns its cost when you want push notifications, saved payment details and a loyalty scheme that brings people back every week.

### How do I get customers to download it?

Ask the people already in front of you. A code on the table, a card in every delivery bag and a first-order reward do more than paid ads for an app nobody has heard of.

### Who handles delivery for orders from my own app?

You choose: your own riders, a third-party delivery company, or pickup and dine-in only. Settle this before you launch, because a slow first delivery loses the customer you worked to move.
